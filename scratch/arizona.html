Arizona Scratchers: what's actually left

54 Arizona games ranked by what they return per dollar right now — not what they were worth the day they were printed.

A Scratcher whose big prizes have already been claimed keeps selling at the same price, with the same headline number on the front. Nothing on the rack tells you the prize you're chasing is already gone.

The Arizona Lottery publishes, for every prize tier of every game, how many prizes were printed and how many are still unclaimed. That is enough to work out exactly what each game has left — so we pull it daily and rank every active game.

Cheap tickets pay back the least

The same pattern turns up in every state we analyse: Arizona climbs from 58 % on $1 tickets to 78 % on $50 ones, the same climb we find in our Texas, California and every other state's data. Eighteen unrelated state lotteries keep making the same design decision: the cheap tickets on the rack are the worst value in the shop. That does not make an expensive ticket a good bet — it is still a guaranteed average loss, just a smaller one per dollar, with far more money at risk per ticket.

How to read this

"Value per $1 now" is the unclaimed prize money divided by what it would cost to buy every remaining ticket. A game showing 70% means players will get back about 70 cents per dollar across the tickets still out there. "At launch" is the same figure for the full print run — what the game was designed to pay.

The gap between those columns is the point: a game well below its launch figure has had its big prizes claimed and is now a worse deal than designed. The "riding on top prize" column is the health warning — where much of the remaining value sits in one tier, the rating hangs on a couple of tickets that may already be sold.

Across every state we analyse, cheaper tickets return less — we tested that on 1,275 games in 18 states and it held in all of them: read the full analysis.

Every Arizona game launches returning roughly 59 % to 78 % of sales as prizes. That is the house edge, and no game escapes it — this table shows which are currently least bad, never which are good. See what expected value means and where your lottery dollar goes.

How we calculate it

Arizona publishes each game's price, overall odds and full prize table with per-tier odds, so the print run is cross-checked across every tier. One Arizona quirk needs care: the state is inconsistent about annuity prizes. Most games list the cash value a winner could take, but a few list the advertised total of all payments instead, with no cash value published anywhere. Those games look entirely plausible — they produce a payout figure well inside the normal range — so we detect them by comparing the top tier against the game's own advertised annuity figure, and drop them rather than publish a number we can't stand behind.

Two honest limitations. Arizona does not publish a percent-sold figure, so we infer it from the share of prizes already claimed; because small prizes often go unredeemed, that understates sales and makes these figures slightly conservative. And "unclaimed" is not the same as "unsold" — a top prize counted as remaining may already sit in a ticket someone bought and hasn't cashed. Late in a print run that gap dominates, so games over 90% sold are flagged and hidden by default.
